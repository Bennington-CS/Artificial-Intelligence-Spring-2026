# Prompt
For the following gobbet: (i) identify where the passage is from; (ii) explain the context of which the passage is a part; (iii) explain the passage in detail. Take as much space as you need.
# Passage
Text generated by an LM is not grounded in communicative intent, any model of the world, or any model of the reader’s state of mind. It can’t have been, because the training data never in- cluded sharing thoughts with a listener, nor does the machine have the ability to do that.
# Sample Answer
This passage is from “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots”, by Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major, and Margaret Mitchell.  The central claim of the paper is that LLMs can produce fluent text, but such fluency can cause us to misunderstand what the systems are actually doing.
A parrot can repeat language-like sounds without understanding what they mean.  A stochastic process is one that works probabilistically.  So the "stochastic parrot" metaphor suggests that language models generate probabilistic sequences of words without understanding those words in the way human beings understand them.  The article claims that LLMs produce output that is apparently, but not actually, meaningful.
The context of the current passage is a discussion of coherence.  LLM output can seem coherent, but such coherence is in fact supplied by us.  When humans read or hear language, we naturally assume someone is attempting to communicate, and that someone has beliefs, intentions, and possibly understanding.  This is the shared context.  For example, if I say, “It’s cold in here,” you do not treat my utterance as a mere string of words; rather, it is an expression of meaning.  Maybe I'm making an observation.  Maybe I'm asking you to close the window or complaining.  You decide on the meaning based on an inference about my mental state.
But LLMs do not have any intent.  They are not trying to communicate an idea to a reader.  Rather, they are producing text based on patterns in training data.  Furthermore, and LLM has no model of the world; and so while it might produce sentences about grief, or tables, or a government, there is no meaning (for the LLM) behind the words.
Finally, the model has no model of the reader's mind.  Human writers do: we might explain something differently, based on whether we are speaking to a novice or an expert; or we might leave out details of an event a friend already knows.  But even when an LLM appears to have a model of the reader's mind, it is merely putting one word after another without understanding what those words mean.
The reason for these shortcomings are twofold.  First, LLMs are trained on symbolic sequences of symbols, and such sequences cannot amount to sharing thoughts.  Second, machines cannot share thoughts because they do not have thoughts.
A possible weakness in the paper's argument is that the inference in this passage is too hasty.  The authors seem to move from the idea that the model is trained on symbolic sequences to the idea that the model cannot be sharing thoughts.  But this step needs more connective tissue.  Written language is also a sequence of symbols, and written language can share thought.  Morevoer, writing never gives us direct access to another's mind; rather, it gives the grounds to infer mind, intention, and meaning.  So training data being symbolic sequences cannot by itself settle the question.
The authors might respond that the second point is decisive: the machine lacks the capacity to share thoughts with a listener, because a machine has no thoughts.  But if so, then the first point is doing less work; and if the decisive issue is the machine’s incapacity, not the symbolic form of the training data, then the argument needs an account of that incapacity.  This objection does not mean that the authors are wrong; but the argument needs to be developed in a less compressed fashion for it to be completely persuasive.
